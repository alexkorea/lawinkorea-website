If you miss the legal deadline to report a change in your alien registration details, a change of address, or a change/addition of workplace, you may receive a **fine notice** from the Immigration Office. Treating it as "just a fine, no big deal" can backfire later — the violation record can resurface when your stay extension or status change is reviewed.

This article explains what to check when you receive a notice and how to respond, based on the Immigration Act and the official fine schedule.

> **The standard fine amounts here are based on an internal compilation (as of 2026-09-22) and current provisions confirmed via the Korea Law Information Center (law.go.kr).** Actual amounts and procedures can vary by immigration office, so always check the details printed on your own notice first.

## 1. What a "beomchikgeum" fine is — the notification disposition system

A beomchikgeum is an amount under the Immigration Act's **notification disposition** system (Article 102 onward): for relatively minor violations, the office head notifies you to pay a set amount instead of filing a criminal complaint. Paying within the deadline closes the case; missing it lets the office file a complaint, moving the matter into criminal-style review (sabeom simsa).

## 2. Three things to check first when you receive a notice

1. **Legal basis and violation period** — Does the cited article (e.g., registration detail change, address change, workplace change/addition report) and the violation period match the actual facts?
2. **Standard fine amount** — Does it match the official schedule for that violation-period bracket?
3. **Payment deadline** — Missing it can trigger further procedures, so confirm this first.

These are all official standard amounts from the fine schedule; the actual amount charged can be adjusted by the relevant office depending on the case (intent, mitigating circumstances, etc.). If your notice looks different from what you expect, don't guess — contact the issuing office directly.

## 4. If you disagree — clarify before you pay

A notification disposition exists to close minor cases quickly, so it doesn't follow the usual administrative appeal/litigation route first.

- **If you pay**: The procedure for that violation closes once you pay within the deadline.
- **If you don't pay**: The office can file a criminal complaint, which can move the matter into investigative and prosecutorial procedure.

So if you dispute the underlying facts (a different calculation of the reporting period, a legitimate reason you couldn't meet the deadline, etc.), it's practically safer to contact the issuing office and submit supporting materials **before** paying, not after. Deciding on your own and letting the deadline pass risks triggering a complaint.

## 5. Does paying affect your visa status?

Even after the fine is paid and the case is closed, the violation record remains and can later be referenced in stay extension, status change, or points-based reviews (e.g., deduction items in the skilled-worker conversion points system). Repeated or more serious violations can be referred into a criminal-style review (sabeom simsa), which can lead to heavier outcomes such as status revocation or a departure order. Criminal fines and immigration administrative dispositions are separate tracks with different decision-makers and procedures. See [Immigration Offense Review Guide](/blog/immigration-offense-review-guide) for the full sabeom simsa process.

## 6. The practical order of steps

1. Check the legal basis, violation period, and amount exactly as printed on the notice.
2. If you don't dispute the facts, pay by the stated method within the deadline.
3. If you dispute the facts or have a legitimate reason, contact the issuing office and prepare supporting materials before paying.
4. If you're concerned about how the record could affect future status reviews, have your full stay history reviewed with an administrative agent office.

Matters likely to move into criminal procedure belong to a separate legal process, while immigration offense review and status response fall within an administrative agent's practice.

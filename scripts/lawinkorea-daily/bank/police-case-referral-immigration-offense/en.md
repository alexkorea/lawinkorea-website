Many people are questioned by police after a crackdown or a call-out, and an overstay or unauthorized job comes to light. Then they hear, "Your case is going to immigration now." In short, the law says police do not handle an immigration offense case to the end. They hand it to the immigration office, which investigates and then chooses among a notified fine, a complaint or deportation proceedings.

## Key points

- An immigration offender is a person recognized as having committed an offense listed in Articles 93-2 through 100 of the Immigration Act (Article 2, item 14).
- If an investigative agency other than an immigration officer opens such a case, it must hand it over without delay to the head of the competent immigration office (Article 101(2)).
- An immigration offense cannot be prosecuted without a complaint from the head of the office (Article 101(1)).
- The office may issue a notified fine, file a complaint, or move to deportation proceedings.

## 1. Why does the case move to immigration instead of staying with police?

The Immigration Act gathers the handling of immigration offense cases in the immigration offices. Two provisions are the core.

- **Article 101(1):** A case on an immigration offense cannot be prosecuted without a complaint from the head of the regional immigration office.
- **Article 101(2):** If an investigative agency other than an immigration officer has opened such a case, it must hand it over without delay to the head of the competent regional immigration office.

So even if the police caught you on the spot, the Immigration Act side of things, such as overstay, unauthorized work or activity outside your status, is investigated by immigration. The result of a handed-over case is reported in writing to the head of the agency that handed it over (Enforcement Decree, Article 103).

A **general criminal case**, such as assault or theft, is different. The criminal process runs through the police, prosecutors and courts, and immigration separately looks at how the result affects your stay. A person sentenced to imprisonment or more and then released falls under a deportation ground (Article 46(1), item 13). We cover that in our [foreign nationals' criminal cases and visas guide](/en/blog/criminal-case-visa-defense).

## 2. What happens after the handover?

An immigration officer may investigate a foreigner suspected of falling under any item of Article 46(1) (the suspect) (Article 47). Points to know at the investigation stage:

- **Summons and questioning:** The suspect may be summoned and questioned, and another immigration officer must take part (Article 48(1) and (2)).
- **Record and corrections:** Statements are written into a record. The officer reads it to you or lets you read it, then asks whether anything is wrong. If you ask to add, delete or change something, that statement must be recorded too (Article 48(3) and (4)).
- **Effect of the record:** The suspect interrogation record in an immigration investigation is treated as a suspect interrogation record under Article 244 of the Criminal Procedure Act (Article 102(4)). So give your statements carefully and truthfully.
- **Inspection of home or belongings:** Your consent is required before officers inspect them or ask for documents or items (Article 50).

Your rights and preparation when giving a statement are covered separately in [Rights to know when you receive a summons](/en/blog/immigration-summons-statement-rights).

## 3. After the investigation: notified fine, complaint or deportation

When the investigation ends, the office decides the direction according to the seriousness of the case.

1. **Notified fine:** When the offense is established, the office may notify you in writing, with reasons, to pay a penalty fine equivalent to a criminal fine (Article 102(1)). The Minister of Justice may also waive the notified fine, considering age and circumstances and the motive and result of the violation (Article 103(2)).
2. **Immediate complaint:** If the investigation shows the circumstances warrant imprisonment or more, the office must file a complaint immediately without a notice (Article 102(3)).
3. **Non-payment and complaint:** If you do not pay within 15 days of receiving the notice, the office must file a complaint (Article 105(1) and (2), main text). No complaint is filed if you paid beforehand, or if a deportation order was issued to the offender (Article 105(2) proviso and (3)).
4. **No double punishment:** Paying as notified means you cannot be punished again for the same case (Article 106).

How to respond to a fine notice is set out in our [guide to responding to fine notices](/en/blog/immigration-fine-notice-response-2026). Even if you pay, residence-related decisions such as deportation or a departure order are made separately.

## 4. When detention (protection) applies

If there are reasonable grounds to suspect a foreigner falls under a deportation ground and the person has fled or may flee, an immigration officer may obtain a detention order and detain the person (Article 51(1)). In urgent cases there is a procedure to detain first, giving the reason, and then show an emergency detention form (Article 51(3) and (4)). Objections and temporary release after detention are covered in our [detention objection guide](/en/blog/immigration-detention-objection-guide).

## 5. What to prepare during the police investigation

1. **Note the date, the agency and the contents of the record.** It helps when you later check your statements at the immigration investigation.
2. **Organize your stay documents.** These include your alien registration card, passport, extension application history and employment contract.
3. **Confirm the handover and the summons schedule.** Keeping the date and place in the summons is the basic rule.
4. **Do not give statements that differ from the facts.** The record can be reviewed again later.
5. **If your authorized period ends during the investigation,** see [Extending your stay during an offense review](/en/blog/offense-review-pending-stay-extension).

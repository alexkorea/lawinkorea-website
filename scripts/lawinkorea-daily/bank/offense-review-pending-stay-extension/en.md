When your stay period is running out in the middle of an investigation, the hard part is deciding whether to apply for an extension or sit tight. The short answer: the rule that an extension must be filed **before your stay period ends** does not change because you are under investigation. Whether it is granted is then decided by the office together with the investigation findings.

## Key points

- Even during an investigation or offense review, file the extension before expiry. No rule I could find extends your period automatically because a case is pending.
- Results fall into roughly three groups: a notice that no violation was found, a disposition such as a notice of fine, and departure-related measures such as a recommendation to depart, a departure order, or a deportation order.
- If an extension is refused, a departure deadline is set, and where there are real obstacles you can ask for that deadline to be deferred or for an extension to leave.
- Disposition records show up separately in later permanent residence and status-change reviews, so look at today's extension and your future plans together.

## The investigation is on, and my expiry date is close

Immigration officers can investigate a foreigner suspected of falling under a ground for deportation, and where needed they can require you to appear and answer questions. The summons must state its purpose, date, and place. If you do not speak Korean, your statement must go through an interpreter, and it is recorded in a written record.

But the investigation and your stay period run on different clocks. A foreigner may stay only within the status and period granted, and to stay longer needs an extension permit before the period ends. The extension application itself must also be filed before the period ends.

A common misunderstanding is this: "I am under investigation, so I am safe until the result comes." Then the expiry date passes. It is hard to find any provision extending the period because of an investigation, and staying past the period without an extension permit is a separate violation. You end up adding a second problem on top of the first.

## What decisions can come out of the review

When the investigation ends, the head of the office promptly examines whether you fall under a ground for deportation and decides. Roughly:

| Decision | When |
|---|---|
| Notice of no violation | If the person is found not to fall under a deportation ground, they are told so |
| Notice of fine (tong-go cheobun, 통고처분) | When the investigation firmly establishes the offense, a written notice sets a fine equivalent to a criminal fine |
| Criminal complaint | If the offense would warrant imprisonment or more, a complaint is filed right away |
| Recommendation to depart | A suggestion to leave voluntarily where the violation is minor |
| Departure order | Can be issued to people who wish to leave voluntarily at their own expense, among others |
| Deportation order | May be issued when a deportation ground is found |

Cases do not all follow one fixed line. For the same kind of violation, residence is sometimes permitted after a notice of fine, and sometimes a notice of fine leads to a departure measure. For example, unauthorized part-time work by a student on a first, minor offense can be handled by a notice of fine followed by a residence permit, while unauthorized work in construction is guided toward a departure order without exception.

One more point. Even where a violation of another law would be a ground for deportation, the permanent residence criteria mention people who received a residence permit (including through the offense review) without a deportation order because of humanitarian or similar reasons. So the review does not always end in departure.

## What happens if I file an extension

File, but do not assume the reviewing office does not know about the case. During residence review, the office can call the applicant in, ask questions, and require materials where needed. Existing permits can also be cancelled or changed where a violation is serious, but the person may be heard first, and the reason, date, and place must be notified at least 7 days before the hearing.

In practice it is better to prepare like this.

- File the extension before the expiry date.
- Prepare a written statement setting out how the violation happened, your remorse, and your plans.
- Gather materials showing your life base in Korea, such as family ties, employment, tax and residence records.

If the extension is refused, the refusal is notified in writing, and the form states a departure deadline within 14 days of issue. If a change of status is refused, the person may instead be allowed to remain in the status already granted, so results differ depending on what was applied for.

If you cannot leave by the deadline, there are two routes. If there is no available transport or a serious reason such as illness, you can apply to defer the departure deadline. And if your permitted period is ending and you cannot leave for an unavoidable reason, you can apply for an extension to leave. Both require materials explaining the reason, and an extension beyond 30 days needs the Minister of Justice's approval.

## Can I work during the review

If you still have stay period left and your status allows work, no provision I found blocks you merely because you are under investigation. Work is always judged by your status and approved workplace.

Two cautions, though.

First, if your period has already ended, everything changes. That is a stay outside the permitted period, so not only working but staying itself becomes a problem.

Second, if the case is about an unauthorized workplace change or work outside your status, continuing that activity extends the same violation. Some statuses, such as Visiting Employment (H-2), also have their own handling standards for violations, so check which standard attaches to your status first.

## How the decision affects later residence permits

The result of an offense review stays as a record in later reviews. A few examples:

- **Permanent residence (F-5)**: Having received fines for immigration violations of KRW 5 million or more in the last 3 years, or combined fines of KRW 7 million or more, is a barrier under the conduct requirement (as of October 2026, Ministry of Justice residence manual). So is a deportation order where 7 years have not passed since departure, or a departure order where 5 years have not passed. In the points system, deductions apply by notice-of-fine amount for dispositions within 5 years of application.
- **Status-change reviews**: For the Job Seeking (D-10) visa and similar, people who received a deportation or departure order within 5 years of applying, or who meet the fine standard, are listed as ineligible.
- **Counting stay periods**: When a long-term resident of another kind changes to Residence (F-2), the periods under a recommendation to depart or a departure order are excluded from the stay period count.
- **Workplace changes**: For a late workplace-change report, a first violation may be allowed after a notice of fine, but with 2 or more violations in the last 2 years, a notice of fine followed by a departure measure is the stated rule.

In the end, "will this extension be granted" and "how will this look in a permanent residence or status-change review 3 or 5 years from now" belong together. If you have any choice in how the case ends, weigh the record it leaves.

## Steps in order

1. Write your permitted stay expiry date in your calendar, separate from the investigation schedule.
2. If you get a summons, check the date and place, and request an interpreter if needed.
3. File the extension before the expiry date. Leaving after applying may lead to refusal, so apply while you are in Korea.
4. Prepare a written statement and supporting materials together.
5. If you receive a notice of fine, it must be paid within 15 days of receipt, and non-payment can lead to a criminal complaint.
6. If you receive a refusal or a departure measure, check the stated departure deadline first, and consider applying for deferral right away if it is hard to meet.
